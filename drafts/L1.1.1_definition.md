# L1.1.1 Definition

**Vulnerability act.** A vulnerability act is a span of talk in which the speaker's own words expose a shortfall of the speaker, or of the speaker's own group, in a matter that teammates can hold the speaker to account for.

**Why it matters.** Such a span gives teammates grounds for a negative evaluation of the speaker. This puts the speaker's face at risk: the image that the speaker claims in the group, such as being capable, credible and reliable. The gates below test this risk from the words. Coders never judge it directly.

## Three elements

A span is a vulnerability act only when all three elements hold. Each element is one gate.
- **V1 · Real shortfall.** The words state a shortfall, or they ask for help or a check with the speaker's own work.
- **V2 · Own shortfall.** The speaker's own words place the shortfall on the speaker or on their own group. A teammate can raise the shortfall, but only the speaker's words can own it.
- **V3 · Interpersonal stake.** Words in the transcript show what the team can expect of the speaker in this matter, so a teammate could hold the shortfall against the speaker.

## Shortfall

**Shortfall.** A shortfall is something that counts against the person or group who has it. V1 decides whether the words state one. V2 decides who has it.
- **Error (claim).** A claim that the speaker gave the team was wrong.
- **Error (work).** Work, a decision or conduct was wrong, or work was not done. A past failure to know or to act also belongs here.
- **Gap.** Something about the speaker's own work is not known or not understood now.
- **Limit.** Something the speaker cannot do or cannot keep up with, now or ahead. A judgment that the speaker's ability falls short also belongs here, for example "I'm just not good at this".
- **Personal difficulty.** The words disclose content on the Personal difficulty list (L1.1.3).
- **Not shown.** Requests only. The speaker asks for feedback or help with their own work but does not say why. The reason could be an error, a gap, a limit or a personal difficulty. The request itself is the vulnerability act.

**Requests.** A request for help or a check with the speaker's own work shows a shortfall, even when the speaker gives no reason. A request does not count when it is an assignment, a task that needs two people by design, a required review or sign-off, an invitation, or a request for an item that the other person owes.

## Two layers

Code every span that passes the gates on two layers (L1.1.2).
- **Kind.** What the speaker exposes: one of the six kinds above.
- **Way.** How it comes out: Volunteered, Answered, Conceded, Requested: feedback or Requested: help.

Also record **Scope**. Episode means one event or task. Trait means a judgment of the speaker as a person or a performer.
  For example [constructed]: "I don't know how to run the report. Can you walk me through it?" This turn has two spans: Gap × Volunteered, and Gap × Requested: help. Both have Scope: Episode.

## What the gates do not test

- **Felt risk, intent and sincerity.** Coders cannot see them. They explain why the act matters, but they never establish a gate.
- **Emotion and tone.** They support the words, but they never establish a gate. A stated feeling is recorded in the Feeling stated field (L1.1.2).

## What this layer leaves out

Code no act for these turns. Other layers or fields cover some of them.
- **A feeling with no shortfall.** For example: "I'm really anxious about this pour." A feeling about other people or about the project is also left out.
- **Trust in a teammate's work.** For example, the speaker delegates a task.
- **A question with no not-knowing words.** For example: "Why do we need the second vapor barrier?"
- **Voice about other people's work or ideas.** For example, a challenge, a concern or a proposal.
- **A shortfall of other people only.** For example: "The sub never sent the drawings."
- **Trouble only in the talk or the meeting.** For example, a slip of the tongue, a formula, or trouble with the call.
